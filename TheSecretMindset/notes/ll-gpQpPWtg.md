## Conceptual Frameworks and Principles (Not Complete Strategies)

### Supply and Demand Imbalance Mapping
Supply zones represent areas where selling pressure overwhelms buying momentum [01:00]. Demand zones represent areas where massive purchase orders cluster [01:00]. Markets seek balance through imbalance via mathematically precise liquidity pathways [00:30]. Market structure follows phases: accumulation, trend development, and distribution [01:30]. Smart money doesn't randomly enter markets but orchestrates entries/exits where maximum market inefficiency exists [01:30]. This is a conceptual framework for understanding market movement, not a tradeable strategy.

### Volume Profile Analysis
Volume profile reveals where markets breathe and transforms flat price charts into living landscapes of trading activity [03:00-03:30]. Point of Control = price level with maximum traded volume, acting as a gravitational center [03:30-04:00]. Smart traders treat Point of Control like a magnetic pole attracting price action [04:00]. Thick volume sections show where massive buy/sell orders clustered; thin sections show market indifference [04:30]. Combine volume profile with Fibonacci retracements for confluence zones [05:30]. This is primarily an analytical tool and framework for identifying institutional positioning.

### Order Flow and Footprint Charts
Retail traders see candles; professionals see order flow [06:30]. Footprint charts show buying volume (right/green side) and selling volume (left/red side) at specific price levels [07:00-07:30]. Color intensity indicates volume level [07:00]. Point of Control marks the highest-volume price level [07:30]. Value Area High/Low contains 70% of all trading volume [07:30-08:00]. Delta shows difference between buying and selling volume (green = more buying, red = more selling) [08:00]. Helps identify where big players place bets [08:30]. This is a tool for understanding market microstructure rather than a complete strategy.

### Contextual Price Movement: Impulse vs. Correction Phases
Impulse movements are strong, directional moves with price trending sharply up or down, characterized by series of candles moving in same direction, often with increasing momentum and high trading volume [09:30-10:00]. Corrective movements are periods where market takes a breather, moving sideways or slightly against main trend, with smaller candles and potentially decreased volume [10:00-10:30]. Understanding whether market is in impulse or corrective phase influences trading strategies [10:30]. Combine this with support/resistance levels or volume profile to determine where phases begin/end [11:00-11:30]. This is a bias filter and contextual framework rather than a standalone strategy.

### Dynamic Support and Resistance Evolution
Support and resistance are dynamic, constantly reshaped by trader participation and market psychology [18:30-19:00]. When price breaks below support zone, it transforms into potential future resistance [19:00]. Broken support becomes resistance because traders who bought at that level are now underwater and may sell [19:00-19:30]. Price congestion plays a huge role in zone evolution; sideways movement creates zones where orders pile up [20:30]. Factors affecting zone strength: 1) Time spent there (longer = stronger) [21:00]; 2) Recency of test (recent = more weight) [21:30]; 3) Magnitude of move away (bigger/faster = stronger rejection) [21:30]. This is a conceptual framework for understanding market memory and support/resistance dynamics, not a specific trade entry rule.

### Contextual Indicator Filtering (Principles)
Technical indicators are supporting tools, not main decision drivers [21:30]. Price remains the core truth [21:30-22:00]. Moving averages, oscillators, and other indicators come from price calculations [22:00]. Match indicator settings to market personality [22:30]; what works for stocks might not work for currencies [22:30]. Markets change their behavior patterns often—during trends vs. ranges [23:00]. Using too many indicators creates confusion; pick a few that complement each other [23:30]. Switching between indicators or constantly changing settings hurts results [24:00]. Let indicators confirm what price already shows you [24:00]. If oscillator shows oversold but price keeps making lower lows, trust price action, not indicator [24:30]. This is a framework for using indicators correctly, not a strategy.

### Alternative Price-Based Chart Types
Tick charts create new bars after a specific number of trades, showing real trading activity rather than time periods [25:00]. Active periods show more bars; quiet periods show fewer bars [25:00]. Renko charts filter out minor price moves, drawing new boxes only when price moves a set amount, focusing on pure price movement [25:30]. Renko excels at showing strong trends and making support/resistance clear [25:30]. Range bars create new bars based on price movement (each bar covers same price range), helping see momentum [25:30-26:00]. These price-based charts show true market activity, unlike time-based charts [26:00]. During major news, time charts miss key market dynamics [26:30]. Tick charts reveal true market heartbeat; professional traders often prefer them [27:30-28:00]. This is a tool selection principle, not a tradeable strategy.
